I am a computer scientist working on trustworthy artificial intelligence, at the intersection of AI, cybersecurity, digital forensics, secure agentic systems, and reliable multilingual language technologies. I am a Lecturer and Coordinator of the Big Data Science programme in the Department of Computer Science at the University of Pretoria. I am also affiliated with the Data Science for Social Impact (DSFSI) lab and am a Faculty Fellow of African Data Science & Artificial Intelligence (AfriDSAI). I hold a Global Perspectives Responsible AI Fellowship at the Stimson Center, supported by Microsoft, where I contribute to the AI & Environment working group.

I earned my PhD in Computer Science from the University of the Witwatersrand, Johannesburg. My doctoral work built tools for the reliable and generalisable forensic analysis of social-media text, including deep-learning methods for authorship attribution. I am Principal Investigator, with Vukosi Marivate, of the AI4D African Languages Lab (IDRC/FCDO, CAD 1.07 million). I am also Co-Principal Investigator of AfriGemma (Google Research Fund, USD 200,000) and of Afirka LLM, open efforts to build African-language large language models. Other support includes the Google exploreCSR programme and Google AI research funding. I have more than 15 years of university teaching and supervision experience. I have taught databases, networking, information security, machine learning, and deep learning, at the Tshwane University of Technology from 2009 to 2018 and at the University of Pretoria since 2019.

Research focus

Secure & trustworthy agentic AI

Making LLM-based agents and retrieval-augmented systems robust, auditable, and safe to deploy, so that they behave reliably when they act on behalf of people.

AI for cybersecurity & digital forensics

Machine learning for authorship attribution, fraud and spam detection, and misinformation and hate-speech analysis: evidence-grade methods that investigators can rely on.

Reliable multilingual language technologies

Open data, models, and evaluation for low-resource and African languages, so that AI systems work fairly and dependably beyond English. This includes Afirka LLM, which I co-lead as Co-PI, and the AfriScience-MT translation work (EMNLP 2026).

  2. NLP J.
    Cross-lingual embedding methods and applications: A systematic review for low-resourced scenarios
    Thapelo Sindane, Vukosi Marivate, and Abiodun Modupe
    Natural Language Processing Journal, 2025
    Abs DOI Bib

    The field of Natural Language Processing (NLP) has achieved significant success in various areas, such as developing large-scale datasets, algorithmic complexity, optimized computing capabilities, refined individual and community expertise, and more, particularly in languages such as English, French, and Spanish. However, such global north unilateral strides have inadvertently created a substantial representation bias towards many languages categorized as low-resourced languages, with the majority being African languages. As a result, rudimentary resources such as stopwords, lemmatizers, stemmers, and word embeddings, as well as advanced multilingual transformer-based models remain under-developed for these languages. Compounding these circumstances is the lack of insights surrounding the development of these resources in the low-resourced context (e.g., how to develop embeddings for morphologically rich languages). Looking back, research priorities aiming to create these resources, largely motivated by the high cost attached to remedying these issues shifted, leading to the rise of alternative methods such as cross-lingual transfer learning (CLTL). CLTL involves transferring domain knowledge gained from supervised training to a domain with limited supervision signals. This study conducts a systematic literature review of CLTL techniques, in the context of cross-lingual models and embeddings, looking at their mathematical foundations, application domains, evaluation metrics, languages covered, and the latest developments. The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the present scenario of CLTL techniques, identifying areas for future research and development to advance cross-lingual natural language processing applications specifically in low-resourced settings.

  6. Appl. Sci.
    Post-Authorship Attribution Using Regularized Deep Neural Network
    Abiodun Modupe, Turgay Celik, Vukosi Marivate, and 1 more author
    Applied Sciences, 2022
    Abs DOI Bib

    Post-authorship attribution is a scientific process of using stylometric features to identify the genuine writer of an online text snippet such as an email, blog, forum post, or chat log. It has useful applications in manifold domains, for instance, in a verification process to proactively detect misogynistic, misandrist, xenophobic, and abusive posts on the internet or social networks. The process assumes that texts can be characterized by sequences of words that agglutinate the functional and content lyrics of a writer. However, defining an appropriate characterization of text to capture the unique writing style of an author is a complex endeavor in the discipline of computational linguistics. Moreover, posts are typically short texts with obfuscating vocabularies that might impact the accuracy of authorship attribution. The vocabularies include idioms, onomatopoeias, homophones, phonemes, synonyms, acronyms, anaphora, and polysemy. The method of the regularized deep neural network (RDNN) is introduced in this paper to circumvent the intrinsic challenges of post-authorship attribution. It is based on a convolutional neural network, bidirectional long short-term memory encoder, and distributed highway network. The neural network was used to extract lexical stylometric features that are fed into the bidirectional encoder to extract a syntactic feature-vector representation. The feature vector was then supplied as input to the distributed high networks for regularization to minimize the network-generalization error. The regularized feature vector was ultimately passed to the bidirectional decoder to learn the writing style of an author. The feature-classification layer consists of a fully connected network and a SoftMax function to make the prediction. The RDNN method was tested against thirteen state-of-the-art methods using four benchmark experimental datasets to validate its performance. Experimental results have demonstrated the effectiveness of the method when compared to the existing state-of-the-art methods on three datasets while producing comparable results on one dataset.
